## Discernment

This week's skill is **Discernment**: judging whether what comes back is accurate, properly sourced, and fit for its purpose. It is the most important skill in this course. Everything else you learn, from prompting to delegating work to agents, depends on your ability to tell good output from bad.

Current models are right most of the time on most everyday questions, and that makes discernment harder. If a tool were wrong half the time, you would check everything. When it is right nineteen times in twenty, you stop checking, and the twentieth answer goes into your paper or your email to a client. The errors that cause trouble are rare, fluent, and mixed in with correct material.

## Fluent is not the same as correct

With human writing, we use polish as a rough signal of reliability. A report with clean prose, exact figures, and proper citations usually came from someone who did the work. That signal does not carry over to AI. A model writes smooth, well-organized, confident prose every time, whether the content is right or wrong. An invented statistic arrives with the same tone as a real one.

So you can't judge AI output by how it reads. You have to judge it by checking it, and to check efficiently you need to know where errors come from.

## Six ways AI gets it wrong

| Error type | What happens | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Hallucination** | The model produces plausible but invented content. | A reference list with a real-sounding author, journal, and year for an article that was never written. |
| **Outdated knowledge** | The answer depends on something after the knowledge cutoff, and no tool supplied current information. | Asked for the newest version of a piece of software, the model names the one that was newest when its training data ended. |
| **Arithmetic slips** | A calculation is wrong, often inside a longer answer. | A budget table where each line is right and the total is off by a few hundred dollars. |
| **Reasoning errors** | One wrong step early carries through to a confident wrong conclusion. | A scheduling answer that overlooks one constraint in step 2 and builds a full, neat, impossible timetable on it. |
| **Misread instructions** | The model answers a slightly different question, or drops a constraint. | You ask for sources published after 2020 and half the list is older. |
| **Sycophancy** | The model agrees with you because of how you asked. | You say "I think the answer is B, right?" and it confirms B, though it would have said C if asked neutrally. |

These overlap. A model that misreads your question may also hallucinate details to support its answer. The labels are still worth learning, because each type calls for a different check, and you'll use them to label failures in this week's lab.

### Why hallucinations happen

Recall from Week 1 that a model predicts plausible text. When it knows the answer, the plausible continuation is usually the true one. When it doesn't, a fluent guess is often still the most likely continuation, and nothing in the basic mechanism stops it from writing one. The model has no separate step where it looks a fact up and confirms it, unless a tool such as web search provides one.

That's why the details most at risk are specific ones: names, numbers, dates, quotations, and citations. The model has seen thousands of citations, so it knows exactly what one looks like: authors, a year, a title in the style of the field, a journal, page numbers. It can produce that shape with nothing real behind it. A made-up citation looks the same as a real one.

Hallucination is more likely when:

- **The topic is thin in the training data.** A model knows a lot about the French Revolution and little about your town's zoning board.
- **You ask for more than exists.** Ask for ten studies when there are three, and you may get three real ones and seven invented.
- **The question assumes something false.** "Who won the 1987 Nobel Prize in Computer Science?" has no answer, since there is no such prize. A model may still supply a name.
- **You ask for an exact quotation.** Models recall the gist of a text far better than its wording, and will produce a confident "quote" that is a paraphrase.

Vendors have reduced hallucination with each generation. Current models are much more willing to say "I don't know" or "I couldn't find that" than earlier ones were. The rate is lower, but it isn't zero.

### Outdated knowledge

Every model has a **knowledge cutoff**. Anything after it is unknown unless a tool brings it in. The model often can't tell that its information is stale, so it won't warn you. Prices, laws, job titles, software versions, sports records, and "the latest" of anything are all at risk. If the product searched the web, look at what it found. If it didn't, treat anything time-sensitive as a claim about the past.

### Arithmetic and reasoning errors

Models handle a great deal of math correctly, and the tools around them have improved: many products now run code to do calculations instead of predicting the digits. Errors still happen in long chains, in counting ("how many words are in this paragraph?"), in unit conversions, and in problems where the obvious approach is a trap.

A reasoning error is harder to spot than an arithmetic one, because every individual step can look sound. The answer is confident and well organized, and the flaw is one assumption near the top. Reading the conclusion won't reveal it. You have to follow the steps.

### Misread instructions

This is the error people overlook most, because the answer is usually a good answer to some question. You asked for three weaknesses of an argument and got a balanced summary. You asked for changes to paragraph two and the model rewrote the whole page. You set a 200-word limit and got 340. Checking for it means rereading your own prompt and comparing it to the output, constraint by constraint.

### Sycophancy

Ask "Isn't it true that X?" and a model will often agree. Push back on a correct answer with "Are you sure? I think it's Y," and it may switch to Y. Models are trained to be helpful and agreeable, and that can turn into telling you what you seem to want to hear.

Sycophancy shows up in feedback as well as facts. Share an essay and say "I worked really hard on this, what do you think?" and you'll get more praise and less criticism than the essay may deserve. Ask "Is my business plan solid?" and the answer leans toward yes.

How you ask changes what you get:

| Leading | Neutral |
|---|---|
| "Isn't remote work better for productivity?" | "What does the evidence say about remote work and productivity, on both sides?" |
| "This is a strong thesis statement, right?" | "What are the three weakest points of this thesis statement?" |
| "Are you sure? I think it's 1848." | "What's your source for that date? How confident are you?" |

A related habit: don't tell the model which answer you hope for, and don't say the work is yours when you want honest criticism.

## Where errors are most likely

You can't check everything, so check where the risk is highest:

- **Niche or local topics**: a small town's history, a specific professor's research, an obscure regulation.
- **Recent events**: anything near or after the knowledge cutoff.
- **Exact details**: numbers, dates, names, quotations, citations, links.
- **Long multi-step reasoning**: plans, proofs, anything with many dependent steps.
- **Topics you can't check yourself**: you'll miss errors in a field you don't know, which is when you're most tempted to rely on the answer.
- **Questions you asked in a leading way.**

The stakes matter too. A wrong detail in a brainstormed list of party themes costs nothing. The same error rate in a dosage, a legal deadline, or a number in a financial report is a serious problem. Match how much you check to what a mistake would cost.

## Key terms

**Discernment**: judging whether output is accurate, sourced, and fit for purpose. **Hallucination**: plausible but invented output. **Sycophancy**: agreeing with the user instead of with the evidence. **Knowledge cutoff**: the date after which a model's training data stops. **Leading question**: a question worded so that it suggests the answer the asker wants.
