# Week 6: Autonomy controls and usage

**COSC-300/500 Applied AI Essentials** · 4D focus: Delegation

## What you will be able to do

- Review an agent's plan before it runs, and change it.
- Set what an agent may do without asking, and recognize irreversible actions.
- Interrupt and redirect an agent, and recover from a wrong turn.
- Review a finished task against the brief.
- Read what a task cost in usage.

## Autonomy controls

Supervising an agent comes down to one question: **how much may it do without asking you?** Agent tools offer a range of answers:

- **Plan first.** The agent researches and proposes a plan. Nothing changes until you approve it.
- **Ask each time.** You approve every action as it comes up.
- **Auto-accept edits.** The agent makes changes without asking; you review afterward.
- **More autonomy.** Fewer checks still, for trusted tasks in safe settings.

| Setting | What you give up | When it fits |
|---|---|---|
| Plan first | A few minutes at the start | Any task that is new to you, or large |
| Ask each time | Your attention throughout | Important files; actions that are hard to undo |
| Auto-accept edits | The chance to stop a bad change before it happens | Routine work on a copy or a scratch folder |
| More autonomy | Most of your oversight | Well-tested tasks where nothing valuable is at risk |

There's no single right setting. A new kind of task on important files deserves a plan and frequent approvals. A routine task on a scratch folder can run with more autonomy. As your trust in a particular task grows, you can loosen the controls.

### Plan before acting

Read an agent's plan the way a manager reads a proposal. Is the goal right? Are the inputs right? Is the order sensible? Is anything missing, or anything included that shouldn't be? **Edit the plan** before approving. This is the cheapest moment to catch a mistake: before anything has changed.

Things to look for in a plan:

- **A step you didn't ask for.** "Delete the duplicates" when you asked only for a list of them.
- **A missing step.** No mention of the index you asked for, or of checking the result.
- **A wrong assumption.** "I'll treat the newest file as correct" when you know the newest one is a draft.
- **The wrong scope.** Files or folders outside what you named.

A useful question to ask yourself is "What would make me refuse this plan?" If you can't think of anything, you probably haven't read it closely.

### Approval fatigue

When a tool asks for approval twenty times in a row, people stop reading and select "yes" each time. Once that happens, the approval step no longer protects you. Guard against it by matching the setting to the task. Let low-risk actions, such as reading files, go through without asking, so that the requests you do see are the ones that matter, and read those.

### Irreversible actions

Pause before approving anything that is hard to undo:

- deleting or overwriting files;
- sending messages or email;
- spending money;
- acting outside the folder you gave it.

Keep backups and work on copies. An agent that makes a mistake on a copy has cost you a few minutes.

### Interrupt and redirect

You can stop an agent mid-task. When it heads the wrong way, stop it, say what's wrong and what to do instead, and let it continue. Recovering well is a skill; starting over every time something goes wrong wastes the work that was right.

## Reviewing the result

When the agent says it's done, the work is ready for review. It isn't finished until you've checked it. The agent's summary of its own work is a claim, and Week 3 applies to it.

- **Go through your acceptance criteria one at a time.** This is why you wrote them.
- **Look at the files themselves.** Open the folder and the outputs. Compare counts: files before and after, rows in and out.
- **Check a sample by hand.** Pick three items and trace each back to its source, as you did with citations in Week 4 and numbers in Week 5.
- **Look for what's missing.** Agents sometimes complete most of a task and describe it as complete. Ask directly: "List anything in the brief that you did not do, and anything you were unsure about."
- **Check for side effects.** Did anything change that shouldn't have?

When you find a problem, tell the agent what you found and let it fix it, then check that fix.

## Usage and context

Every task uses part of your plan's **usage allowance**. On Claude Pro, usage is measured over a rolling five-hour session and over a week. Large folders and long tasks also fill the agent's **context window**, the same working memory you learned about in week 2.

What makes a task expensive:

- **How much the agent reads.** A folder of 300 PDFs costs far more than a folder of 10.
- **How many steps it takes.** Each step sends the growing context again.
- **The model and effort level.** Larger models and more thinking use more of the allowance.
- **Rework.** A vague brief that leads to two failed attempts costs three runs.

A clear brief that names the inputs is the cheapest way to cut usage, because the agent doesn't have to explore to find out what you meant.

After a task runs, check what it cost. Over time you'll develop a sense of which tasks are cheap and which are expensive, and how the way you write a brief affects the cost.

## Key terms

**Plan mode**: an agent proposes a plan and makes no changes until it's approved. **Approval**: your permission for a specific action. **Irreversible action**: one that can't easily be undone. **Usage allowance**: how much work your plan allows in a period.
