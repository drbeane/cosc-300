## What you will be able to do

- Write a prompt that states the goal, context, constraints, audience, and output format.

- Recognize common prompting advice that no longer helps.

## Description

This week's 4D skill is **Description**: how you communicate the task. A model cannot read your mind. It sees only what is in its context: your prompt, the conversation so far, anything you've attached, and some instructions you never see. If something important isn't there, the model fills the gap with a guess. Most disappointing AI output traces back to a description that left too much to guess.

A useful test is to imagine handing your prompt to a capable new coworker who has never met you, has not seen your course or your job, and cannot ask a follow-up question. Would they have what they need? If they would have to guess who the reader is, how long the result should be, or what you plan to do with it, so will the model. The model's guesses are usually sensible, which is why a vague prompt gives you something generic instead of something wrong.

## Anatomy of a strong prompt

A strong prompt usually covers five things:

| Part | What it answers | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Goal** | What do you want, and why? | "Summarize this article so I can decide whether to read the whole thing." |
| **Context** | What background does the model lack? | "I'm a sophomore in a marketing course. We have covered segmentation but not pricing." |
| **Constraints** | What limits apply? | "Under 150 words. Don't use the word 'synergy'. Keep every number from the original." |
| **Audience** | Who will read the result? | "This goes to a client who has no technical background." |
| **Output format** | What shape should it take? | "A table with one row per option and columns for cost, time, and risk." |

Not every prompt needs all five. A quick factual question needs none of them. The longer or more important the task, the more of them are worth writing down.

**The goal includes the reason.** "Shorten this email" and "Shorten this email, because my manager reads on her phone between meetings" lead to different results. The second tells the model what kind of shortening you need: a clear first line and no scrolling. When the model knows why, it can make good choices about details you didn't think to specify.

### Before and after

Compare these two prompts.

> **Weak:** Summarize this article.

> **Strong:** Summarize this article for a busy department chair who is deciding whether to fund a pilot program. Use five bullets and stay under 120 words. Lead with the cost and the strength of the evidence. Flag any claim the article makes without support.

The second prompt isn't longer for the sake of it. Each added sentence removes a guess:

| Added sentence | The guess it removes |
|---|---|
| "for a busy department chair who is deciding whether to fund a pilot program" | Who is reading, and what decision the summary supports |
| "five bullets and stay under 120 words" | Length and format |
| "Lead with the cost and the strength of the evidence" | What matters most |
| "Flag any claim the article makes without support" | What to be skeptical about |

## Techniques that help

Each technique below fixes a particular kind of problem. Reach for one when you see that problem in an output.

### Be clear and direct

Say exactly what you want. People often hint, as they would with a person: "This paragraph feels a bit long?" A model may answer the question ("Yes, it is fairly long") without doing anything. "Cut this paragraph to three sentences and keep the statistic" gets the edit.

Say what you want done, more than what you want avoided. "Don't be so formal" leaves a wide range of tones open. "Write the way you'd explain this to a friend over coffee" names the target.

### Use structure

When a prompt contains both your request and material to work on, mark which is which. Otherwise the model can mistake a sentence in your pasted notes for an instruction, or the reverse. Headings work, and so do tags, which are labels in angle brackets that you make up:

> I'm applying for the job described below. Using my resume, draft three bullet points for the top of my cover letter. Use only experience that appears in the resume.
>
> `<job_posting>`
> (paste the posting here)
> `</job_posting>`
>
> `<resume>`
> (paste the resume here)
> `</resume>`

The tag names are not special. Any clear label works, as long as you use the same one to open and close a section.

### Give examples

One or two examples of a good output show format, tone, and level better than a paragraph of description. If you want quiz questions in a certain style, paste two you like and ask for ten more like them. This technique has its own section below, because it is the most powerful one on the list.

### Set a role and audience

"You are reviewing this as a hiring manager at a small accounting firm" focuses the response on what that person would care about. A role works because it packs a lot of context into a few words: what the reader knows, what they value, and what they would find unconvincing. It works best paired with a real task. "You are an expert" by itself adds little.

### Ask for uncertainty

"Tell me which parts you're least sure of" invites the model to flag weak spots instead of smoothing over them. So does "If the notes don't say, write 'not stated' instead of guessing." Models tend to produce a complete-looking answer even when the information isn't there, and a direct instruction is the simplest way to counter that.

### Ask for questions first

For a task where you aren't sure what the model needs, let it tell you:

> Before you write anything, ask me up to five questions about anything you'd need to know to do this well.

This turns a one-shot prompt into a short interview, and it often surfaces a requirement you hadn't thought about.

## Why examples work

Models continue patterns. When your prompt includes an example of what you want, the model picks up its format, length, and tone and continues in kind. This is called **in-context learning**: the model learns from the prompt itself, with no retraining. You may also see it called **few-shot prompting**, where each example is a "shot".

Here is a prompt that describes the output:

> Write flashcards for these biology terms. Keep them short and make the back of the card an example, not a definition.

And one that shows it:

> Write flashcards for the biology terms below, in the same style as these two.
>
> Front: Osmosis
> Back: A raisin swells when you soak it in water, because water moves in through its skin.
>
> Front: Catalyst
> Back: Yeast makes bread dough rise faster and is still there at the end.

The second prompt is easier to write, and its results will match what you had in mind more closely.

Two cautions:

- **The model copies flaws too.** If your example has a mistake or an odd habit, expect to see it again.
- **It can copy too closely.** With a single example, every output may come back the same length with the same sentence shapes. Giving two or three examples that differ from one another shows the model which features matter and which are free to vary.

## Advice that no longer helps

Prompting advice spreads online and stays in circulation long after the models change. Some habits you may have picked up:

- **Magic phrases.** Lines such as "take a deep breath" or offers to tip the model came from tests on older models. They do little on current ones. Clear content beats clever wording.
- **Shouting.** Writing an instruction in capitals, or repeating it three times, can make a current model apply it too rigidly and neglect the others. If one instruction matters most, say so in a normal sentence and give the reason.
- **Keyword strings.** "cover letter marketing entry-level professional" is a search query. Models respond better to full sentences that explain the situation.
- **The perfect first prompt.** You don't have to get everything into one message. Reading the first output and replying with a correction is often faster than planning for every case in advance. The last topic this week covers how to revise in an orderly way.

## Key terms

**Prompt**: the text you give a model to tell it what you want. **In-context learning**: learning a pattern from examples in the prompt, with no retraining. **Few-shot prompting**: including a few examples of the desired output in the prompt.
