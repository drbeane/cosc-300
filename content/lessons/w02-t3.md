## Standing context

The last topic showed that a new chat starts empty and that everything in the context shapes the response. Two features put context there **every time**, without your retyping it:

- **Custom instructions** (called preferences in some products): something you write once about who you are and how you want responses.
- **Memory**: information the tool saves from your past conversations and brings into new ones.

Neither changes the model. Both work by adding text to the context at the start of a chat, in the same way the system prompt is added. They enter quietly, which is their strength and their risk.

## Custom instructions

Custom instructions are a short piece of writing that the product includes in every new chat. You write them in the product's settings, and you can change them whenever you like.

Good instructions cover two things: **who you are** and **how you want answers**.

> I'm a junior nursing student who also works part-time as a pharmacy tech. When I ask about a clinical topic, assume I know basic anatomy and pharmacology.
>
> Start with a one- or two-sentence answer, then give the detail. Use plain language. When you give a drug dose or a lab value, say that I should confirm it in a current reference. If my question is ambiguous, ask me before answering.

Notice what that example does:

- It gives a **role and background**, so the model can pitch its explanations at the right level.
- It states **format preferences** that apply to almost any question.
- It includes a **behavior** the student wants every time: ask when unsure, and flag numbers to verify.

Instructions like these apply the lessons of the first topic once, so you don't have to repeat them in every prompt.

Things to avoid in custom instructions:

- **Rules for one task.** "Always answer in APA format" is useful in a paper and strange when you ask for a dinner recipe.
- **Long lists.** Every line is read on every chat. The more rules there are, the less weight each one gets. A short paragraph or two is enough.
- **Instructions that fight each other.** "Be extremely thorough" and "keep it under 100 words" leave the model to pick one.

## Memory

Memory lets a product carry information from one conversation into later ones. Products do this in two main ways, and many offer both:

| Kind | How it works | What you see |
|---|---|---|
| **Saved memories** | The tool writes down short facts about you, such as "Is a nursing student" or "Prefers tables", and adds them to future chats. | A list in settings that you can read, edit, and delete. |
| **Reference to past chats** | The tool searches or summarizes your earlier conversations and pulls in what seems relevant. | Usually less visible. You may see a note that past chats were used. |

In either form, the mechanism is the one you already know: text is added to the context. The model has not been retrained on you. What it "remembers" is whatever the product chose to insert for this chat.

Memory is useful. You don't have to reintroduce yourself, and a tool that knows you're preparing for a specific exam can connect today's question to last week's. It also has costs:

- **Leaks between tasks.** A detail from one conversation can turn up in an unrelated one. If you once asked for help with a friend's wedding toast, the tool may bring up the wedding while you're drafting a lab report.
- **Stale facts.** Memory saved in September ("is looking for an internship") may be wrong by January and will still shape answers.
- **Wrong inferences.** The tool decides what to save, and it can misread you. One question about a medical condition doesn't mean you have it.
- **Hidden influence.** When an answer seems oddly tailored, memory may be the reason, and you won't always be told.
- **Privacy.** Whatever is stored about you sits in your account. Anyone who can open your account can read it.

### What belongs there, and what doesn't

| Good candidates | Poor candidates |
|---|---|
| Your role, field, and level | Details of a single assignment |
| Format preferences you want almost every time | Passwords, account numbers, ID numbers |
| Constraints that always apply ("I use a Mac", "I write in British English") | Health, financial, or legal details you wouldn't want to resurface |
| Long-running projects you return to often | Information about other people |

### Know what's stored about you

Open your memory settings and read what's there. This is worth doing a few times a term.

:::steps Reviewing memory in most chat products
1. Open your account menu and select **Settings**.
2. Find the section for memory or personalization. Custom instructions are usually on the same page or the one beside it.
3. Read the saved memories. Delete any that are wrong, out of date, or more personal than you'd like.
4. Check whether the tool is set to refer to your past chats, and decide whether you want that on.
5. Find the control for a temporary or incognito chat, which doesn't use memory and isn't saved to it.
:::

You have three levels of control:

- **Edit or delete** single memories.
- **Use a temporary chat** (also called incognito) for a conversation you don't want remembered or influenced by memory.
- **Turn memory off** entirely.

A fourth option is simply to tell the tool, "Forget that" or "Remember that I prefer short answers." Check the settings afterward to confirm that it did.

Making a habit of this is part of responsible use. The tool's picture of you shapes every answer it gives.

## Voice

Voice mode lets you talk instead of type, and hear the reply read aloud.

| Voice works well for | Typing works better for |
|---|---|
| Thinking out loud about an idea that isn't formed yet | Precise instructions with several constraints |
| Practicing an explanation, a presentation, or an interview answer | Long prompts with pasted material |
| Being quizzed before an exam | Anything with code, numbers, or names that must be exact |
| Language practice | Results you need to copy, review, or cite |
| Times when your hands or eyes are busy | Private topics in a public place |

Voice changes the conversation in a few ways. Spoken prompts tend to be longer and looser, which is often good: people give more context when they talk than when they type. Replies are usually shorter and more conversational, and a table or a list doesn't translate to speech. Speech recognition also makes mistakes, especially with names and technical terms, so check the transcript before relying on what was said.

A useful pattern is to **talk first and type second**. Spend five minutes describing a messy idea out loud, then ask for a written summary of what you said and continue in text.

## Key terms

**Custom instructions**: standing instructions you write about yourself and your preferences, added to every chat. **Memory**: information a tool saves about you and brings into later chats. **Temporary chat**: a conversation that doesn't use memory and isn't saved to it; also called incognito. **Standing context**: context that is added to every conversation without your typing it.
