## What you will be able to do

- Use AI at each stage of writing: planning, drafting, revising, and editing.
- Tell apart uses that extend your thinking from uses that replace it.
- Ask for feedback in a way that gets honest criticism.
- Keep your own voice in a piece that AI helped with.
- Apply the course's expectations on authorship and disclosure.

## From answers to grounded work

For three weeks you have worked with a model in a plain chat: you ask, it answers from what it learned in training. This week begins the second stage of the course, where the model works from **your sources and files**. Writing is the place to start, because it is the knowledge work you already do most, and because it shows the central question of this stage clearly: which parts of the work are yours?

## AI at each stage of writing

| Stage | What AI can do | Example prompt |
|---|---|---|
| **Planning** | Help you find and test an angle, ask you questions, propose structures | "Here's my topic and what I think I want to argue. Ask me five questions that would help me sharpen the argument." |
| **Drafting** | Turn your notes or outline into a first version to react to | "Turn these bullet points into a rough first paragraph. Keep my claims and my order. Don't add new ones." |
| **Revising** | Find gaps in the argument, suggest a better order, point out what a reader would miss | "What would a skeptical reader object to in section 2?" |
| **Editing** | Tighten sentences, fix grammar, adjust tone and length | "Cut this to 200 words. Keep the statistic and the last sentence." |
| **Summarizing** | Reduce a long document to what matters for your purpose | "Summarize this report for someone deciding whether to adopt its recommendation." |

The quality of the help depends on how much of your own material the model has. Ask for a paragraph on a topic with no notes, and you get the average paragraph on that topic. Give it your outline, your notes, and the two points you care most about, and the draft is built from your thinking.

## The thinking is still yours

In most college and professional writing, the document is evidence of thinking: that you understood the reading, weighed the evidence, and reached a position you can defend. A model can produce the document without any of that happening. So the useful question about any use of AI in writing is **what it does to your thinking**.

| Uses that extend your thinking | Uses that replace it |
|---|---|
| "Find the weakest step in my argument." | "Write my argument." |
| "Shorten this to 200 words without losing the main point." | "Add some citations." |
| "What would a skeptical reviewer ask?" | "Summarize this paper I haven't read." |
| "I'll explain this concept. Tell me what I got wrong." | "Explain this concept so I can paste it in." |

The right-hand column isn't forbidden. It's risky, and the risks are specific:

- **"Write my argument"** gives you a position you didn't reach and may not be able to defend when someone asks a follow-up question.
- **"Add some citations"** invites hallucinated references, as you saw in Week 3. Even real ones are attached to claims after the fact, without anyone having read them.
- **"Summarize this paper I haven't read"** leaves you unable to check the summary, and you'll repeat any error in it with full confidence.

A simple test: when the piece is done, could you explain and defend every claim in it without the tool open? If you could, the thinking is yours.

## Getting honest feedback

Feedback is one of the best uses of AI for writing, and sycophancy is the obstacle. Ask "Is this good?" and you'll usually hear that it is. To get criticism you can use:

- **Ask for problems.** "List the three biggest weaknesses in this draft, most serious first."
- **Give it a reader.** "Read this as a hiring manager who has 40 applications to get through. Where would you stop reading?"
- **Give it the standard.** Paste the assignment prompt or the rubric and ask how the draft measures up against each item.
- **Ask for the reader's experience.** "After each paragraph, tell me in one sentence what you think my point is." Where the model's sentence doesn't match your intent, a human reader would be lost too.
- **Ask for comments before rewrites.** "Don't rewrite anything. Tell me what to fix and why." You learn more from making the change yourself, and the words stay yours.

Then apply Discernment to the feedback. Models sometimes flag things that are fine, or push every piece of writing toward the same safe, balanced tone. You decide which comments to take.

## Keeping your voice

AI-edited prose drifts toward a recognizable style: even, polite, a little formal, heavy on tidy three-item lists and sentences that announce what they are about to say. Readers increasingly notice it. If you ask a model to "improve" your writing with no other direction, that style is what you get, and what made the writing yours gets lost.

Ways to keep your voice:

- **Write the first draft yourself** when the piece matters, and use the model for feedback and editing.
- **Limit the edit.** "Fix grammar and cut repetition. Don't change my word choices or sentence structure otherwise."
- **Show it your writing.** Paste a sample of your own work and ask it to match that style. This is the examples technique from Week 2.
- **Ask what changed.** "List each change you made and why." Then reject the ones you don't like.
- **Do a final pass yourself**, reading aloud. Change anything you wouldn't say.

## Responsible use

Writing with AI raises four questions.

- **Authorship: whose ideas and words are these?** There's a range, from spell-checking through feedback and line editing to text the model drafted. Different settings draw the line in different places. A marketing team may be happy with AI-drafted copy. A writing course that is teaching you to draft is not.
- **Disclosure: have you said how AI was used?** A disclosure should be specific enough that a reader knows what the tool did. "I used Claude to suggest a structure for section 2 and to edit the final draft for length. The argument and the sources are my own" is a useful statement. "AI was used" is not.
- **Plagiarism.** Model output can closely echo the wording of its sources, and if you paste a source in and ask for a paraphrase, the result still needs a citation. Ideas need credit whether or not AI reworded them.
- **Copyright and confidentiality.** Think about what you upload (a textbook chapter, a classmate's draft, a client's document) and what your privacy settings from Week 1 allow the vendor to do with it.

This course asks you to disclose AI use in everything you submit. Other courses and employers have their own rules, which can differ from one assignment to the next. Finding out which rules apply is your job, and "the tool let me" is not a defense.

## Key terms

**Thought partner**: using AI to test and extend your own thinking instead of to produce the finished work. **Disclosure**: a statement of how AI was used in a piece of work. **Authorship**: responsibility for the ideas and words in a piece of work. **Voice**: the recognizable style of a particular writer.
