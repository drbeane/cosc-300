## 1. AI as a writing partner

AI can help at every stage of writing:

- **Drafting**: getting past the blank page with a first version to react to.
- **Editing**: tightening, restructuring, adjusting tone, fixing flow.
- **Thinking**: testing an argument, finding counterpoints, asking what a skeptic would say.
- **Summarizing**: turning a long document into the parts that matter for your purpose.

The thinking is still yours. The most valuable uses push your thinking further; the riskiest replace it.

| Good uses | Risky uses |
|---|---|
| "Find the weakest step in my argument." | "Write my argument." |
| "Shorten this to 200 words without losing the main point." | "Add some citations." |
| "What would a skeptical reviewer ask?" | "Summarize this paper I haven't read." |

The risky column isn't forbidden; it's risky. "Add some citations" invites hallucinated references (week 3). Summarizing a paper you haven't read leaves you unable to check the summary.

## 2. Projects

A **project** is a persistent workspace around one body of work. It holds:

- **Instructions** that apply to every chat in the project: your role, the audience, the format, the citation style.
- **Reference files**: a knowledge base of documents the model can draw on.
- **Project memory**: what the model learns across the project's chats, kept separate from your other work.
- **Many chats**, all sharing that context.

### When a project beats a one-off chat

Use a project for work that runs across many sessions with the same sources and the same expectations: a course you're taking, a research paper, a job search, an ongoing client. You set up the context once instead of re-explaining it every time. For a single question, just open a chat.

## 3. Guided practice: a project for your research topic

Using the sources you brought:

1. Create a project for your research topic.
2. Write project instructions: your research question, who the brief is for, and the citation style you want.
3. Upload your 5–10 sources.
4. Ask the project to map where the sources agree and disagree.
5. Pick one claim in its answer and check it against the source yourself.

> **Instructor note.** Step 5 is the point. Ask a few students to report whether the claim they checked held up.

## 4. Responsible use

Writing with AI raises questions about **authorship** (whose ideas and words these are), **disclosure** (saying how AI was used), **plagiarism** (AI text can closely echo its sources), and **copyright** (what you upload and what you publish). The course policy asks you to disclose AI use; other courses and employers have their own rules, and you need to know them.
