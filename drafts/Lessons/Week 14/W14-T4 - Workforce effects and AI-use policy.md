## Workforce effects

AI tends to change **tasks** before it changes **jobs**. Much routine drafting, summarizing, and first-pass analysis is already shifting to AI, and the people in those roles spend more of their time specifying, supervising, and checking.

A job is a bundle of tasks. Take a marketing coordinator: drafting posts, resizing images, pulling weekly numbers, answering routine email, planning a campaign, managing a relationship with a difficult client. AI handles the first four increasingly well, and helps much less with the last two. The job continues, and its content shifts toward the parts that need judgment, context, and trust.

| Tasks moving toward AI | Tasks staying with people |
|---|---|
| First drafts of routine documents | Deciding what should be written, and for whom |
| Summarizing and reformatting | Judging whether the summary is right and what it leaves out |
| First-pass analysis of data | Choosing the question, and answering for the conclusion |
| Routine code | Specifying, reviewing, and owning what ships |
| Answering common questions | Handling the unusual case and the upset person |

The right-hand column is this course. Each row on the right is Delegation, Description, Discernment, or Diligence.

How large and how fast these effects will be is uncertain, and careful researchers disagree. Predictions of sweeping change within a few years and predictions of slow, uneven adoption are both made by serious people. Treat a confident forecast, in either direction, as you'd treat any confident claim: ask what evidence it rests on.

One effect deserves particular attention. Entry-level work is often the most exposed to automation, and it's also how people have traditionally learned a profession. Organizations will need to think about how newcomers build expertise when the tasks that used to teach them are delegated.

Checking AI output well requires knowing what good work looks like, and people have traditionally learned that by doing the routine work themselves. If you've never written a contract summary by hand, it's harder to spot what a generated one got wrong.

For you, this suggests a deliberate approach to your first years of work:

- **Learn the underlying work**, even where a tool could do it for you. Do some of it by hand, and have the tool check you instead of the reverse.
- **Build expertise in a field.** Discernment depends on knowing the subject. The combination of real knowledge of a field and skill with these tools is worth more than either alone.
- **Take the tasks AI is weak at**: the ambiguous, the interpersonal, the ones where someone must be accountable.
- **Keep learning the tools.** The specific products in this course will change. The habits of testing, verifying, and supervising will carry over to whatever replaces them.

The skills you've practiced this term—writing a clear brief, reviewing a plan, checking output, limiting access, evaluating a system—are the skills this kind of work requires.

## Organizational AI-use policy

Most organizations now need a policy on AI use. Without one, each employee decides alone what's acceptable, and the decisions differ. A policy tells people what they may do, so that they can use the tools with confidence, and what they may not, so that the organization's data and reputation are protected. A good one covers:

- **Scope**: who and what it applies to.
- **Approved tools**, and which kinds of accounts.
- **Data rules**: what may and may not be put into AI tools.
- **Disclosure**: when people must say AI was used.
- **Accountability**: who signs off on AI-assisted work.
- **Review**: when the policy will be revisited.

### What each section looks like

Here are sample lines for a small accounting firm.

| Section | Sample wording |
|---|---|
| **Scope** | "This policy applies to all staff and contractors, and to any use of AI tools for firm work, on any device." |
| **Approved tools** | "Staff may use AI tools on the firm's enterprise accounts, listed on the intranet. Personal accounts may not be used for firm work." |
| **Data rules** | "Public and internal information may be used with approved tools. Client financial records may be used only with tools marked 'client data approved' on the list. Social Security numbers and bank account numbers may not be entered into any AI tool." |
| **Disclosure** | "Tell the reviewing partner when AI drafted or analyzed any part of client work. Client-facing documents don't need an AI label, and must be reviewed by a licensed staff member." |
| **Accountability** | "The staff member who signs a piece of work is responsible for its accuracy, however it was produced. Every figure must be verified against source documents." |
| **Review** | "The managing partner owns this policy and will review it every six months." |

Notice what makes these usable. They say what staff **may** do as well as what they may not. They name kinds of data by level, as in the data governance topic. They put the list of specific products somewhere that can be updated without rewriting the policy. And they name a person.

A policy should be short enough to be read. One page that people follow does more than twenty pages nobody opens.

### Common mistakes

- **Banning everything.** People use the tools anyway, without guidance.
- **Naming specific products** in the core rules. The products change within months; the principles shouldn't.
- **No data rules**, which is where most real harm happens.
- **No owner**, so the policy never gets updated.
- **Vague wording.** "Use AI responsibly" tells nobody what to do on a Tuesday afternoon with a client file open.
- **Rules nobody can follow.** "Verify every word of AI output" will be ignored. "Verify every figure and every citation" can be done.
- **Written without the people who do the work.** They know which tasks the tools help with and where the rules will chafe.

Banning everything deserves a second look, given the last two topics. A ban doesn't stop use. It moves use onto personal accounts with consumer terms, out of sight, which is the shadow AI problem. A policy that approves a decent tool and sets clear data rules is safer than one that forbids everything.

### Test your draft

Read your draft policy as three people would:

- **A new employee** on their first day. Could they tell what they're allowed to do?
- **Someone in a hurry**, with a deadline and a client file. Does the policy answer their question in under a minute?
- **Someone looking for a loophole.** What does the policy fail to cover?

Then try it on two cases from your capstone organization. "Can I paste this customer's complaint into the assistant to draft a reply?" "Can I use it to summarize the board minutes?" If the policy doesn't give a clear answer, revise it.

Your capstone includes a **one-page draft AI-use policy** for the organization in your project.

## Key terms

**AI-use policy**: an organization's rules for how its people may use AI tools. **Disclosure**: saying when and how AI was used in a piece of work. **Policy owner**: the person responsible for keeping a policy current. **Task**: one activity within a job, as distinct from the job as a whole.
