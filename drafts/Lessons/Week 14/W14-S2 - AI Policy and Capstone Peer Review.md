## 1. Workforce effects

AI tends to change **tasks** before it changes **jobs**. Much routine drafting, summarizing, and first-pass analysis is already shifting to AI, and the people in those roles spend more of their time specifying, supervising, and checking.

One effect deserves particular attention. Entry-level work is often the most exposed to automation, and it's also how people have traditionally learned a profession. Organizations will need to think about how newcomers build expertise when the tasks that used to teach them are delegated.

The skills you've practiced this term—writing a clear brief, reviewing a plan, checking output, limiting access, evaluating a system—are the skills this kind of work requires.

## 2. Organizational AI-use policy

Most organizations now need a policy on AI use. A good one covers:

- **Scope**: who and what it applies to.
- **Approved tools**, and which kinds of accounts.
- **Data rules**: what may and may not be put into AI tools.
- **Disclosure**: when people must say AI was used.
- **Accountability**: who signs off on AI-assisted work.
- **Review**: when the policy will be revisited.

### Common mistakes

- **Banning everything.** People use the tools anyway, without guidance.
- **Naming specific products** in the core rules. The products change within months; the principles shouldn't.
- **No data rules**, which is where most real harm happens.
- **No owner**, so the policy never gets updated.

Your capstone includes a **one-page draft AI-use policy** for the organization in your project.

## 3. Capstone peer review

You'll work in groups of three. Read each draft against the rubric and give written feedback to **two** classmates. Plan for about 15 minutes per draft.

### Rubric focus

- Is the **task and its users** clear?
- Is the choice of **tools and surfaces** justified?
- Does the **prototype** work, and is there evidence?
- Is the **evaluation** convincing? Would it catch a real failure?
- Are the **risks** named, with realistic mitigations?
- Is the **AI-use policy** specific to this organization?

### Giving good feedback

- Be specific: point to the section you're commenting on.
- Give one real strength and two improvements.
- Suggest; don't rewrite their work for them.
- Ask the question a skeptical manager would ask before adopting this workflow.

> **Instructor note.** Circulate and read feedback as it's written. Push vague comments ("looks good") toward specifics.
