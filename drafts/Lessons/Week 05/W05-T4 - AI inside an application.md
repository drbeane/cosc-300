## Embedded copilots

Every tool so far in this course has been a place you go: you open the chat app and bring your work to it. An **embedded copilot** is AI built into an application you already use, so that it works on what's open in front of you. You'll find one in email, word processors, spreadsheets, slide tools, meeting software, note apps, and design tools. Microsoft calls its version Copilot, and Google's is Gemini in Workspace.

On Maryville accounts, the example is **Copilot in Outlook**. It can summarize a long email thread or help draft a reply, right where the email is.

## What embedded AI is good at

| Application | Typical tasks |
|---|---|
| Email | Summarize a long thread. Draft a reply. Adjust the tone or length of a draft. |
| Word processor | Draft from an outline. Rewrite a selected passage. Summarize a long document. |
| Spreadsheet | Explain or write a formula. Suggest a chart. Describe trends in a table. |
| Slides | Build a first deck from a document. Tidy a slide's layout. Write speaker notes. |
| Meetings | Summarize what was said. List decisions and action items. |

These tasks share three features. They are short, they work on a single item that is already open, and you can see the result right away. The advantages follow:

- **No copying and pasting.** The email is already there. So is the formatting, and so is the recipient.
- **The context is supplied for you.** Much of the Description work from Week 2, pasting in the thread and explaining who's involved, is done by the application.
- **The result lands where it's needed**, as a draft in the reply box or text in the document.
- **It runs under your organization's account.** A copilot provided through your school or employer is usually covered by that organization's data agreement, which a personal chat account is not. That is often why an employer approves it for work material.

## What it can't see

The limit comes from the same place as the convenience. An embedded copilot mostly sees that one application's context: this thread, this document, this sheet. It generally doesn't know about the project plan in another file, the conversation you had in person, or the decision made in last week's meeting, unless your organization's version can search across your files and you ask it to.

Two more limits to know:

- **You get fewer choices.** In a chat app you pick the model, the effort level, and the tools. An embedded copilot usually picks for you, and what you get depends on the license your organization bought. Two people at different companies can have quite different features under the same name.
- **A button is a short prompt.** "Summarize" and "Draft reply" send a brief, general instruction on your behalf. The result is generic for the same reason a vague prompt gives a generic result. Most embedded tools also have a box where you can type. Use it: "Draft a reply that declines the Thursday meeting, proposes Monday or Tuesday afternoon, and keeps it to three sentences" gets a far better draft than the button.

## Inside the app or a separate assistant?

| Inside the app | A separate assistant |
|---|---|
| Quick tasks on what's in front of you | Work that draws on many sources |
| No setup | Projects, files, agents |
| Limited to that app | Broader tools and models |
| The app chooses the model | You choose the model and effort |
| Covered by your organization's agreement | Depends on your own account and settings |

Some examples of the choice:

- **Catching up on a 30-message thread before a meeting.** Use the copilot in your email. It's one click, and the thread is the whole context.
- **Replying to a routine request.** Use the copilot, with a typed instruction that says what you want to say.
- **Writing a proposal that draws on three reports, last year's budget, and the client's emails.** Use a separate assistant with a project. The work spans sources that no single application holds.
- **Working out why a spreadsheet formula returns an error.** Start with the copilot in the spreadsheet, since it can see the cells.
- **Analyzing a messy dataset and checking the cleaning steps.** Use a tool with code execution, as in the second topic this week, where you can read the code.

Many people use both: the embedded tool for quick work in place, and a separate assistant when the task needs more context or more control.

## Check before it goes out

Embedded tools make it easy to send something you haven't read closely, because the draft appears in the reply box looking finished. The habits from Week 3 still apply:

- **A summary can miss the one message that matters**, or state a decision the thread never reached. If you'll act on a summary, open the messages it refers to. Meeting summaries have the same problem and can also attribute a comment to the wrong person.
- **A drafted reply can make commitments.** It may agree to a date, promise a deliverable, or answer a question with a guess. Read every sentence as something you are about to say.
- **Tone can be off** for the person you're writing to. The model doesn't know your relationship with them.

You're responsible for what you send, whoever or whatever drafted it. The recipient sees your name on it.

## Why this matters for your career

For most graduates, an embedded copilot in Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace will be the first AI they meet at work. It may be the only one their employer allows for company data. Knowing what it does well, where it falls short, and when to reach for something else is a practical skill from the first week of a job.

## Key terms

**Embedded copilot**: AI built into an existing application, working on what is open in it. **License**: the agreement that sets which features an organization's users get.
